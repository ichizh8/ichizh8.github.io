When Selection Becomes Search

What abundant generation changes when evaluation can close the loop

A tempting story about generative AI says that once producing competent-looking work becomes cheap, judgment becomes the scarce resource. The machine supplies options; the human supplies taste, wisdom, and the final word. The story preserves a flattering division of labor. It is not yet a general law.

In bounded studies, generative AI reduced completion time and raised ratings on professional writing tasks, while a customer-support rollout study estimated an average productivity increase after agents received access to an AI assistant, with heterogeneous effects by experience and skill (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, 2025). These results make cheaper generation a serious premise, but do not tell us what happens to every difficulty that follows.

The next question is evaluability. If candidates can be tested quickly against a stable objective, failed trials can be discarded, and measured results can guide another attempt, selection can enter an automated search loop. If the objective is disputed, the test is incomplete or gameable, feedback is slow, or errors cannot be reversed, candidate abundance can instead increase the burden of verification.

Even that distinction is incomplete. A system may be able to find a high-scoring result without having the mandate to act on it. We therefore need two ideas: search closure and release closure. Search closure concerns whether generation, evaluation, rejection, and recycling can form a valid loop. Release closure concerns whether a selected result may be imposed on the world under legitimate and accountable authority. The first does not imply the second.

1. The false conservation law of difficulty

Cheap generation is a genuine change, but “cheap” needs discipline. It can mean less time to complete a defined task, more measured output per hour, or a higher rating under an experimental rubric. It does not automatically mean less verification, less rework, better long-term learning, or lower total cost. The writing experiment and support-agent study measured bounded forms of performance, not the full process of knowledge work (Noy and Zhang, 2023; Brynjolfsson, Li, and Raymond, 2025).

Abundance also has more than one level. In an experiment on short-story writing, access to generative AI improved ratings of individual stories, especially for less creative writers, while AI-assisted stories became more similar to one another (Doshi and Hauser, 2024). Better individual outputs and a less diverse collective portfolio can coexist. More competent candidates are not necessarily a wider search of the possibility space.

Four definitions prevent the argument from drifting. Generation produces candidates. Evaluation assigns information about their quality. Selection uses that information to decide which candidates survive or shape the next attempt. Judgment is broader: it includes deciding what quality means, whether an evaluator is valid here, what happens when objectives conflict, and whether a result is ready to leave the search process.

Once these functions are separated, there is no reason to expect a conservation law of difficulty. Lower generation cost can swamp a costly review process. It can also make a reliable evaluator more useful by supplying more candidates to test. If evaluation becomes executable, candidate-by-candidate selection may itself be automated. Difficulty can move, shrink, or change form. “Judgment becomes scarce” is only one possible outcome, and scarcity would require evidence the current studies do not provide.

2. Search closure

Herbert Simon described rational choice under limits as a process of search rather than exhaustive optimization. An agent cannot inspect every alternative, so it searches until it finds an acceptable one under its constraints (Simon, 1955). Generative systems alter the cost and scale of producing alternatives, but Simon’s account does not predict their effects. It gives us a baseline: more candidates help only through the process that tests and retains them.

Search closure becomes possible when six conditions align. Feedback is fast and legible enough to inform the next attempt. The objective is stable enough to encode. The evaluator tracks that objective reliably and resists gaming. Trials are repeatable and reversible. Experimental errors are bounded rather than imposed as large, hidden, or irreversible losses. Finally, the current task falls inside the validated boundaries of both the generator and evaluator.

This is an analytical model, not a validated universal taxonomy. Its point is to explain why cheaper generation can have opposite consequences. With fast feedback, a stable target, a valid evaluator, reversible trials, bounded errors, and known task membership, the system can generate, score, discard, and try again. With slow feedback, plural objectives, brittle tests, path dependence, consequential errors, or uncertain task membership, more candidates may produce more unresolved verification.

Search closure is local. It need not cover a profession, a product, or even an entire workflow. A creative task may contain testable subproblems. A technical task may contain disputed objectives. One stage may support automated selection while problem formulation, evaluator challenge, and release remain outside the loop. The question is not whether a domain is “for machines” or “for humans.” It is whether this candidate, under this objective and evaluator, can be rejected or retained through valid feedback.

3. When the score can close the search loop

FunSearch shows what abundant generation can do when evaluation is deliberately executable. The system paired a frozen pretrained language model for code with an evaluator supplied in the problem specification. Users provided an evaluate function that scored proposed programs on specified inputs and an initial program that could include a skeleton encoding known structure. The model generated variations in the selected part of that program (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024).

This was not “ask a model and trust the answer.” Candidate programs were executed and scored. Programs that failed to run within time or memory limits, or produced invalid outputs, were discarded. Programs that executed within the limits and produced valid outputs entered the database with their scores. Higher-scoring programs were favored in later prompts, while an island-based population method maintained diversity and periodically repopulated weaker islands from stronger survivors (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). Generation and selection were connected by feedback.

The loop produced measured results. On the cap-set problem, FunSearch found previously unknown constructions, including an improved asymptotic lower bound. On online bin packing, it found heuristics that improved on established baselines for the distributions studied (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). The organizationally interesting feature is not that a language model made a mathematical suggestion. Executable evaluation turned imperfect suggestions into a search process that rejected invalid candidates and recycled stronger ones.

Human expertise did not disappear. People supplied the problem, evaluator, program structure, inputs, and interpretation (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). The authors report that FunSearch currently works best with an efficient evaluator, rich scoring feedback rather than a binary signal, and a useful skeleton with an isolated part to evolve (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). Those conditions define the case, not an implementation footnote.

FunSearch did not test whether adding human candidate review would improve or harm the loop (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). A review step was not part of the reported search mechanism, but that does not make reviewers generally redundant. FunSearch is an existence case for search closure in deliberately evaluable mathematical and algorithmic settings. It is not evidence that evaluation is generally cheap, or that automated search lowers total organizational cost.

4. Who decides whether the score applies?

A reliable evaluator still leaves a prior routing problem: does the present task belong to the class for which that evaluator is valid? This problem has two interacting parts. Generator capability concerns whether the system can produce useful candidates. Evaluator validity concerns whether the test detects the losses and improvements that matter. A strong generator can exploit a weak proxy. A strong evaluator cannot rescue a candidate space that omits necessary solutions. Neither question answers the other.

The boundary can be jagged. In a field experiment with consultants, access to an AI system improved speed and quality on tasks designed inside its capability frontier but reduced performance on a task outside that frontier (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026). The result is bounded to that model and task set, yet it warns against assuming that performance transfers smoothly between nearby-looking tasks.

Delegation research likewise treats productive human-AI allocation as partly dependent on knowing the relative capabilities of person and system, rather than on generalized trust (Fügener et al., 2022). I call the needed design competence frontier-sensitive control. It means stating the relevant loss, checking whether the generator and evaluator cover it, routing the task, applying disconfirming tests near a boundary, escalating exceptions, and revisiting the classification when conditions change. This is a proposed framework, not a construct validated by the frontier or delegation studies.

“Keep a human in the loop” is too crude as a design principle. A systematic review and meta-analysis of 106 experiments found that human-AI combinations performed worse on average than the better standalone human or AI component, with effects differing by task type (Vaccaro et al., 2024). The established point is nonautomatic complementarity. The study does not establish override, routing error, or delay as general causal mechanisms.

Those mechanisms remain hypotheses to test locally. Candidate review might catch evaluator failures, or it might add no useful information. An escalation point might improve safety even if routine approval does not. The answer depends on the workflow, interface, reviewer competence, incentives, and failure structure. Appropriate reliance means matching reliance to demonstrated capability under current conditions while monitoring for change (Lee and See, 2004). Human presence alone proves neither control nor complementarity.

5. Prediction is not permission

Pretrial release introduces the functions that search closure cannot settle. Prediction estimates an outcome. Authority identifies who may impose a decision. Accountable release connects action to a person or institution answerable for constraints, challenge, correction, and remedy. Release closure asks whether a selected result may be acted upon under such a mandate.

Using New York City administrative data, researchers compared judicial decisions with machine risk predictions through counterfactual analysis. They found that machine predictions could improve the trade-off between detention and crime risk relative to observed judicial decisions (Kleinberg et al., 2018). Professional authority did not guarantee predictive superiority in that analysis.

But the workflow does not have a FunSearch-like evaluator. Detention prevents observation of the outcome that would have followed release, so outcomes for detained defendants had to be addressed counterfactually (Kleinberg et al., 2018). More fundamentally, a predicted probability of crime does not specify how to value releasing someone who later offends against detaining someone who would not. Prediction can inform a consequence. It cannot derive the legitimate balance among liberty, public safety, legal constraints, and unequal error burdens from accuracy alone.

Nor is detention a reversible experimental trial. Time spent in custody cannot be restored by later learning. Accuracy cannot confer coercive authority, identify who may act, or create a route for challenge. The remaining judgment is institutional: define permissible objectives, specify rights and losses, establish legal constraints, assign authority, and keep someone answerable for correction and remedy. The study does not show that judges possess unique intuition or that an algorithm should independently decide release (Kleinberg et al., 2018).

The cases can now be compared dimension by dimension. FunSearch had an encoded objective, executable feedback, disposable invalid trials, bounded experimental consequences, and a task deliberately structured around its evaluator (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). Pretrial release has contested losses, counterfactual feedback, irreversible burdens on people, legal constraints, and coercive authority that predictive accuracy cannot supply (Kleinberg et al., 2018). FunSearch illustrates search closure. Pretrial release shows why search closure, even if technically improved, cannot by itself produce release closure.

These are diagnostic endpoints, not evidence about prevalence. They clarify the distinction by contrast. They do not tell us how many organizational workflows resemble either case, or how many contain a mixture that changes from stage to stage.

6. The practical-scope objection

The strongest objection is not that search closure is impossible. It is that search-favorable workflows may be rare outside deliberately constructed domains. Fluent reports, legal clauses, treatment suggestions, and strategies may become easy to generate while correctness, safety, legality, integration, and appropriateness remain expensive to establish. More candidates then create more chances for plausible errors to pass shallow checks. If this pattern dominates consequential work, the thesis remains logically valid but loses much of its organizational importance.

That objection should be conceded wherever its conditions hold. A cheap proxy that omits a consequential loss is not a valid evaluator. A fast test that can be gamed is not reliable. Reversibility in a sandbox does not justify experimentation after deployment. And a score that ranks outcomes cannot determine who is entitled to choose among competing harms.

Prevalence is unknown. FunSearch demonstrates possibility under explicit conditions (Romera-Paredes et al., 2024). The jagged-frontier experiment warns that apparent capability may fail to transfer to a nearby task (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026). Neither establishes how often durable search closure can be achieved once integration, monitoring, changing objectives, distribution shift, correlated failures, rework, and liability enter the accounting.

The empirical program follows directly. Map workflows stage by stage. Measure generation, evaluation, integration, escalation, and correction. Stress-test the evaluator near boundaries and after distributional change. Compare automatic selection with meaningful alternatives, including independent checks and exception review. Then ask separately whether the resulting action has release closure. Without such work, both “automate everything” and “put a human everywhere” are slogans.

7. Put judgment where each closure fails

Organizations should stop asking whether every AI output needs a human glance. They should first ask whether the search can close: Is the objective stable? Does the evaluator capture it? Are generator and evaluator validated for this task? Are trials reversible and errors bounded? Can feedback improve the next attempt without exporting the experiment’s costs to others?

Where local evidence supports those conditions, automating candidate selection may be reasonable. The decision should still include integration cost, ongoing monitoring, distribution shift, correlated failure, and the value of an escalation point. A successful pilot does not validate a permanent boundary. Routine review should be retained, redesigned, or removed according to what it demonstrably contributes, not because human presence is presumed either magical or obstructive.

Then ask whether release can close: Who may authorize action? Which rights and losses constrain it? Can affected parties challenge the result? Who must correct errors and provide remedy? No reliability score answers those questions by itself. A valid evaluator can support accountable decision-making, but it cannot confer a mandate.

Where search closure fails, greater practical weight may remain on objective setting, evaluator challenge, boundary recognition, and verification. Where release closure fails, the unresolved work includes legitimate authorization, contestability, correction, and remedy. Whether any of these functions captures greater economic value is an open empirical question. The cited evidence does not establish market value, organizational scarcity, or a labor-market shift.

What remains difficult is not “human judgment” in the abstract. Sometimes it is building a valid test. Sometimes it is recognizing that the test no longer applies. Sometimes it is deciding which losses are permissible. Sometimes it is establishing who may act and who must answer afterward. Abundant generation changes the problem most when selection can become search. It does not make search closure universal, and it never turns prediction into permission.
